Baby Reflux Diet: A Guide for Parents

Gastroesophageal reflux (GER), often called reflux or spitting up, happens when food or milk comes back up from your baby's stomach into the esophagus. Reflux is very common in healthy babies and usually goes away on its own as the sphincter muscle fully develops (Source: NIH).

When Is Reflux a Concern?

While reflux is common, Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a more serious and long-lasting type of reflux. It can cause feeding problems, discomfort, or other symptoms that affect your baby's growth or sleep (Source: NIH). Babies are more likely to have reflux or GERD if they were born too early (premature), have lung problems, or have certain conditions (Source: NIH).

Dietary Changes for Reflux

Thickening Feeds

For formula-fed babies, your doctor may recommend an anti-regurgitation formula (pre-thickened) or adding a thickener (like rice cereal) to formula. Always consult a doctor before thickening feeds. Note that breast milk cannot be thickened with cereal due to the presence of amylase, which breaks down the thickener.

Cow's Milk Protein Elimination

Reflux symptoms can mimic cow's milk protein allergy. Your doctor may suggest:

For formula-fed babies: switching to an extensively hydrolyzed formula for 2–4 weeks.

For breastfed babies: the mother removing cow's milk and potentially other allergens (like eggs) from her diet.

Smaller, More Frequent Feedings

Overfeeding is a key risk factor for reflux. Give smaller, more frequent feedings to prevent the stomach from overfilling.

When to See a Doctor

If your baby has reflux that does not improve, or if they have any of the following signs, it's important to consult a pediatrician:

Poor weight gain and growth (Source: NIH)

Reflux that does not get better with medicines (Source: NIH)

Trouble swallowing (Source: NIH)

Food getting stuck in the esophagus (Source: NIH)

If you are worried about your baby, call your health care provider right away.
