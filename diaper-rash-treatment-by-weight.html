Diaper Rash: Treatment and When to Call the Doctor

Understanding Diaper Rash

A rash is an area of irritated or swollen skin. Many rashes are itchy, red, painful, and irritated. Some rashes can also lead to blisters or patches of raw skin (Source: NIH | Rash). Diaper rash is a common type of contact dermatitis that occurs where the skin has touched an irritant—in this case, urine, stool, or the diaper material itself.

Contact dermatitis causes redness, itching, and sometimes small bumps. You get the rash where you have touched an irritant, such as a chemical, or something you are allergic to (Source: NIH | Rash).

How Rashes Develop

Some rashes develop right away. Others form over several days. Although most rashes clear up fairly quickly, others are long-lasting and need long-term treatment (Source: NIH | Rash).

Because rashes can be caused by many different things, it's important to figure out what kind you have before starting treatment (Source: NIH | Rash).

Diaper Rash and Constipation

Constipation means having fewer than three bowel movements a week. The stool (poop) can be hard and dry. Sometimes it is painful to pass (Source: NIH | Constipation). When a baby is constipated, they may have infrequent but large, hard stools that can irritate the diaper area and worsen diaper rash.

There are things you can do to help prevent constipation:

Eating more fruits, vegetables and grains, which are high in fiber (Source: NIH | Constipation)

Drinking plenty of water and other liquids (Source: NIH | Constipation)

Getting enough exercise (Source: NIH | Constipation)

Taking time to have a bowel movement when you need to (Source: NIH | Constipation)

Important: Using laxatives only if your health care provider says you should. Ask your provider if medicines you take may cause constipation (Source: NIH | Constipation).

If your baby's bowel habits change, check with your provider (Source: NIH | Constipation).

Diaper Rash and Diarrhea

Diarrhea is loose, watery stools (bowel movements). You have diarrhea if you have loose stools three or more times in one day (Source: NIH | Diarrhea). Diarrhea is a common cause of diaper rash because the frequent, watery stools irritate the skin.

The most common causes of diarrhea include:

Bacteria from contaminated food or water (Source: NIH | Diarrhea)

Viruses such as the flu, norovirus, or rotavirus—with rotavirus being the most common cause of acute diarrhea in children (Source: NIH | Diarrhea)

Parasites, which are tiny organisms (Source: NIH | Diarrhea)

Acute diarrhea usually lasts about one or two days, but it may last longer. Then it goes away on its own (Source: NIH | Diarrhea).

Diaper Rash Treatment

Home care for diaper rash includes:

Frequent diaper changes – keep the area clean and dry

Gentle cleansing – use warm water and a soft cloth; avoid wipes with alcohol or fragrance

Air exposure – allow the diaper area to air dry completely before putting on a new diaper

Barrier creams – use diaper rash creams containing zinc oxide or petroleum jelly to protect the skin

Avoid irritants – stop using any wipes, diapers, or creams that seem to worsen the rash

When to Call the Doctor

Call your pediatrician if your baby's diaper rash:

Does not improve with home care within 2 to 3 days

Worsens despite treatment

Has blisters or raw skin – especially if oozing, crusting, or spreading

Is accompanied by fever – especially if the fever is very high

Is accompanied by diarrhea lasting more than a few days – this may be a sign of a more serious problem. Diarrhea that lasts at least four weeks (chronic diarrhea) can be a symptom of a chronic disease (Source: NIH | Diarrhea)

Is accompanied by constipation that changes your baby's bowel habits (Source: NIH | Constipation)

Spreads beyond the diaper area – to the thighs, abdomen, or back

Is very painful – causing your baby to cry excessively during diaper changes

Emergency Signs Requiring Immediate Care

Seek emergency medical evaluation if your baby's diaper rash is accompanied by:

Signs of dehydration – fewer wet diapers (less than 4 in 24 hours), no tears when crying, sunken eyes, dry mouth, or lethargy

Difficulty breathing – rapid breathing, wheezing, or grunting

Swelling of the face, lips, or tongue – this could indicate a severe allergic reaction

Purple or dark red spots that do not blanch (turn white) when pressed

Confusion or unusual drowsiness – difficulty waking or acting abnormally

A stiff neck – this could indicate meningitis

A seizure

High fever with a rash – this combination can indicate serious infections

Persistent Symptoms

Contact your pediatrician if your baby has:

Chronic diarrhea lasting at least four weeks (Source: NIH | Diarrhea)

Constipation with changes in bowel habits – check with your provider if bowel habits change (Source: NIH | Constipation)

Rash that keeps coming back – this may indicate a chronic skin condition or allergy
