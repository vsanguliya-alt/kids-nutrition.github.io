Starting Solid Foods: A Guide for Parents

Introducing solid foods is an exciting milestone in your child&#x27;s development. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends introducing solid foods around 6 months of age, when your baby is showing signs of readiness (Source: NIH). This is a critical step in their development.

Is Your Baby Ready for Solids?

Every baby is different, but there are some common signs that indicate your baby is ready for solid foods:

Being able to sit up with minimal support.

Having good head control.

Showing interest in food by opening their mouth when food is offered.

Being able to move food from the front of the mouth to the back.

First Foods: What to Offer

Start with a single-ingredient, iron-rich food. Good first foods include:

Iron-fortified infant cereal mixed with breast milk or formula.

Pureed meat, poultry, or fish.

Pureed beans or lentils.

Pureed vegetables like sweet potatoes, carrots, or peas.

Pureed fruits like bananas, apples, or pears.

Introducing Allergenic Foods

If you introduce one new food at a time, you will be able to identify any foods that cause allergies in your baby (Source: NIH). When babies can eat foods that contain peanuts depends on their risk of food allergies:

Most babies can have peanut products when they are about 6 months of age (Source: NIH).

Babies who have mild to moderate eczema have a higher risk of food allergies (Source: NIH).

Important Safety Tips

Introduce foods one at a time. Wait 2 to 3 days before introducing a new food.

Avoid choking hazards. Certain foods that can cause choking, such as hard candy, popcorn, whole nuts, and grapes (unless they are cut into small pieces). Don&#x27;t give your child these foods before age 3 (Source: NIH).

Avoid cow&#x27;s milk before age 1, since it does not have all of the nutrients that babies need and babies cannot digest it (Source: NIH).

Do not give your baby honey before 1 year of age (Source: NIH).

When to See a Doctor

If your baby has trouble swallowing, especially with solid foods, or if solid foods are getting stuck in the esophagus, consult your pediatrician. These can be signs of a more serious condition (Source: NIH).
