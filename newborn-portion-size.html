Newborn Portion Sizes: A Guide for Parents

Feeding a newborn can be confusing, especially when it comes to portion sizes. Unlike older children and adults, a newborn&#x27;s stomach is tiny, and their needs change rapidly in the first few weeks. This guide offers a general framework for understanding appropriate feeding amounts for your newborn.

Why Portion Sizes Are Different for Newborns

A newborn&#x27;s stomach is about the size of a cherry and grows to the size of a large egg by day 10. This means they cannot eat large volumes of food in one sitting. Newborns need to feed frequently—often 8 to 12 times a day—because they can only take in small amounts at a time.

General Guidelines for Newborn Feeding Amounts

There is no one-size-fits-all approach, but these general guidelines can be a helpful starting point.

First Few Days

Breastfed babies: In the first few days, your baby will receive colostrum, which is rich in nutrients and antibodies. Newborns may only take about 1 to 2 ounces (30 to 60 ml) per feeding.

Formula-fed babies: In the first few days, a newborn may only take about 1 to 2 ounces (30 to 60 ml) per feeding.

First Week

Breast milk or formula: About 2 to 3 ounces (60 to 90 ml) per feeding, 8 to 12 times per day.

Total: About 16 to 24 ounces (480 to 720 ml) per day.

First Month

Breast milk or formula: About 3 to 4 ounces (90 to 120 ml) per feeding, 8 to 12 times per day.

Total: About 20 to 28 ounces (600 to 840 ml) per day.

How to Know If Your Newborn Is Getting Enough

Rather than focusing on the exact measurement, it&#x27;s often more useful to pay attention to your baby&#x27;s hunger and fullness cues. A baby who is eating enough will:

Have steady weight gain and follow their own growth curve on the pediatrician&#x27;s growth charts.

Have at least 6 wet diapers per day by day 5.

Have regular bowel movements (at least 3 to 4 per day in the first month).

Seem content and satisfied after feedings.

Important Safety Tips

Avoid overfeeding: Give your baby the amount of formula or breast milk recommended.

Avoid cow&#x27;s milk before age 1, since it does not have all of the nutrients.

Do not give your baby honey before 1 year of age.

Babies should not drink juice before age 1.

When to See a Doctor

If you are concerned that your newborn is not eating enough, is not gaining weight appropriately, or is showing signs of dehydration, speak with your pediatrician. If you are worried about your baby, call your health care provider.
