Is 18-Month-Old Starting Solid Foods Normal? What Pediatricians Say

At 18 months, a child should be well into eating solid foods. However, if your child is still struggling with texture or has not yet transitioned to table foods, you may wonder if it's normal. Here is what pediatricians consider typical development at this age and when to be concerned.

What Is Normal for an 18-Month-Old?

By 18 months, a toddler's development includes several areas, including fine motor skills like feeding themselves with a spoon and gross motor skills like walking and running (Source: NIH | Infant and Newborn Development: MedlinePlus – Infant and Newborn Development). In terms of eating, it is expected that an 18-month-old:

Eats a variety of solid foods in small, bite-sized pieces, including meats, vegetables, fruits, grains, and dairy.

Feeds themselves with their fingers and can handle a spoon, though they may still be messy.

Chews and swallows well, having moved past the gagging phase typical of early solids.

Drinks from a cup independently or with minimal help.

Has a predictable eating routine of three meals and one to two snacks per day.

When Is It Not Normal? (Red Flags)

You should be concerned if an 18-month-old is:

Not eating any solid foods or is still exclusively on purees or liquids.

Gagging or choking frequently on soft or small pieces of food.

Refusing an entire food group (e.g., all vegetables or all proteins) consistently.

Not gaining weight or losing weight.

Having difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), which may present as coughing or choking during meals.

Vomiting after eating certain foods, which could be a sign of an allergy or intolerance (Source: NIH | Eosinophilic Esophagitis | MedlinePlus – Eosinophilic Esophagitis).

What Pediatricians Say

Pediatricians emphasize that there is a wide range of what is considered "normal" in child development (Source: NIH | Infant and Newborn Development: MedlinePlus – Infant and Newborn Development). However, by 18 months, the transition to family foods should be well underway. If your child has not made this transition, it is important to seek guidance.

Here are common reasons for eating difficulties at 18 months:

Picky eating: This is a common phase but should not result in a severely limited diet.

Developmental differences: Some children are more cautious with new textures.

Oral-motor issues: Difficulty chewing or swallowing may indicate an underlying motor problem.

Medical conditions: Food allergies, reflux, or other conditions can cause feeding difficulties.

What to Do

Offer a variety: Continue to expose your child to different foods and textures.

Make mealtime positive: Avoid pressure and keep mealtimes relaxed and enjoyable.

Consult your pediatrician: If you have concerns about your child's eating, development, or growth, talk to your pediatrician. They may refer you to a feeding specialist if needed.

The Bottom Line

While it is normal for an 18-month-old to be a picky eater or have food preferences, it is not normal for them to be completely reliant on purees or to have significant difficulty with solid foods. If you have any concerns, speak with your pediatrician.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor about my 18-month-old's eating habits?

Call your pediatrician if your child is not eating any solid foods, is gagging or choking frequently, is not gaining weight or losing weight, or is vomiting after eating (Source: NIH | Eosinophilic Esophagitis | MedlinePlus – Eosinophilic Esophagitis).

Q: How long does it take for a child to transition to solid foods?

Most children transition from purees to table foods between 9 and 12 months of age. By 18 months, they should be eating a variety of solid foods.

Q: What home remedies help with picky eating?

Offer new foods alongside familiar favorites, involve your child in meal preparation, and avoid pressuring them to eat. Limit sugary snacks and drinks between meals.

Q: Does teething affect eating at 18 months?

Yes, teething can cause discomfort and lead to temporary changes in eating habits, but it should not last more than a few days.

Q: Can an 18-month-old have a food allergy?

Yes. Food allergies can cause vomiting, rash, swelling, or difficulty breathing after eating certain foods. If you suspect an allergy, contact your pediatrician (Source: NIH | Eosinophilic Esophagitis | MedlinePlus – Eosinophilic Esophagitis).

Q: When should I go to the ER for a feeding problem?

Go to the ER immediately if your child has severe difficulty breathing, choking, or has a severe allergic reaction (hives, swelling, difficulty breathing). These are emergency signs. For other concerns, contact your pediatrician.
