Constipation in Toddlers: When to Call the Doctor

Understanding Constipation

Constipation means having fewer than three bowel movements a week (Source: NIH | Constipation). In toddlers, it can be a common and uncomfortable problem. While it often resolves with simple dietary and lifestyle changes, there are specific signs that indicate it is time to seek medical advice.

Prevention and Home Care

There are many things you can do to prevent constipation, including:

Eating more fruits, vegetables and grains, which are high in fiber (Source: NIH | Constipation).

Drinking plenty of water and other liquids (Source: NIH | Constipation).

Getting enough exercise.

Taking time to have a bowel movement when you need to (Source: NIH | Constipation).

It&#x27;s not important that you have a bowel movement every day (Source: NIH | Constipation). However, if you are worried about your toddler, you should call your health care provider right away (Source: NIH | Toddler Health).

When to Call the Doctor

Contact your child&#x27;s healthcare provider if the constipation is accompanied by:

Severe or persistent abdominal pain (Source: NIH | Toddler Health).

Blood in the stool or stools that are black and tarry.

Fever (Source: NIH | Toddler Health).

Stools containing blood or pus.

Constipation lasting more than a few weeks, even with dietary changes.

Weight loss or your child is not gaining weight as expected.

Your child is in significant pain or is crying while trying to have a bowel movement.

Your child is not drinking enough fluids or shows signs of dehydration.

Your child is avoiding using the toilet due to fear of pain (which can lead to a cycle of worsening constipation).

When to Seek Emergency Care

Go to the nearest emergency room or call 911 if your child has constipation and also experiences:

Severe, sudden abdominal pain that does not go away.

Vomiting, especially if the vomit is green or looks like feces.

A swollen or hard abdomen.

Signs of dehydration (e.g., no wet diapers for 6 hours, sunken eyes, no tears).

When to Talk to Your Doctor About Medication

If dietary changes do not help, your doctor may recommend a medication. Do not give your child any laxatives without first talking to their doctor (Source: NIH | Constipation). Your pediatrician can recommend a safe and appropriate treatment plan for your child.
